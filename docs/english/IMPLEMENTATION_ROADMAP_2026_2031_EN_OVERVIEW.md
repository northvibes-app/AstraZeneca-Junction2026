## Official English abstract (original page 4; reproduced in substance)

The implementation roadmap guides the gradual implementation of the National Cancer Strategy during its first five-year period (2026–2031). It aims to develop human-centred services, reduce cancer burden through prevention and early detection, and ensure effective and equal treatment.

Although cancer treatment in Finland is of a high standard, the increasing cancer burden and the aim to achieve treatment outcomes comparable to other Nordic countries require significant reforms. Individualised diagnosis and treatment open new opportunities while increasing the need to strengthen the evidence base and improve the impact of prevention, care and research.

The roadmap specifies measures, schedules, responsible actors and resource requirements. Some activities are already part of wider initiatives, while others require additional funding and legislative changes. The roadmap estimates approximately **EUR 140 million in additional investment** for implementing the national strategy as a whole, with expected benefits from avoided cancer cases, earlier detection and increased R&D and innovation. A monitoring group will coordinate and evaluate implementation. A broader review and next-phase roadmap are planned in 2030.

## Implementation framing (original pages 7–8)

The Ministry of Social Affairs and Health published Finland's first National Cancer Strategy for 2026–2035 in November 2025. The roadmap details its first practical implementation phase from 2026 through 2031, including responsibilities, required resources and timelines. It was prepared under the coordination of FICAN, with participation by STM, THL and cancer organisations.

## Equity in implementation (original pages 9–14)

### Socioeconomic factors (pages 9–11)

Long-term studies indicate differences between population groups in cancer incidence, mortality and survival. The document discusses:

- Variation in health literacy, ability to find and interpret information and access to digital services.
- Barriers related to fees, medicine and travel costs, service locations and accessibility.
- Potential differences in screening participation, time to diagnosis, treatment access and rehabilitation.
- The coexistence of public health centres, private providers and occupational healthcare; differences between these pathways can affect timely access.
- The need for accessible plain-language information and services that reach diverse population groups.

The roadmap cites an unpublished study finding substantially longer time to diagnosis for lymphoma patients entering through public care compared with private pathways. **This is described as unpublished research in the original; it must not be presented as an independently verified national rate.**

### Regional disparities (pages 12–14)

Regional variation remains in cancer incidence, outcomes and relative survival. The pathway from initial symptoms through primary-care assessment, further examinations, specialised care and treatment can involve multiple contacts and delays. Long travel distances and limited transport options may create barriers for people living far from major specialist centres.

The roadmap notes that some patients travel hundreds of kilometres to reach central or university hospitals. At the same time, concentration of specialised services can improve outcomes, making coordinated local access and centralised expertise both relevant to planning.

The intended outcome is **equitable access to cancer diagnosis and treatment regardless of place of residence**.

### Older patients (pages 14–15)

Population ageing is expected to drive much of the increase in cancer cases. Older and frail patients need appropriately individualised evaluation that considers functional ability, comorbidities, treatment burden and benefit. The roadmap highlights gaps in geriatric oncology training and coordination.

## Main sections of the roadmap (original contents pages 5–6)

### Goal 1 — Participation and human-centred services
- 1.1 Patient and family participation in service development
- 1.2 Respectful, individualised interaction and active patient involvement
- 1.3 Accessible health communication and health literacy

### Goal 2 — Prevention and early detection
- 2.1 Reduce risk factors through public policy
- 2.2 Local and regional cancer prevention
- 2.3 Effective screening aligned with European recommendations
- 2.4 Legislation and registry data for cost-effective, risk-based screening
- 2.5 Earlier detection throughout the healthcare system

### Goal 3 — Equitable and effective cancer care
- 3.1 National guidelines and care pathways
- 3.2 National quality registry and systematic outcomes assessment
- 3.3 Rehabilitation, psychosocial support, social work and palliative care
- 3.4 Functional capacity in older patients' care decisions
- 3.5 Adequate skilled workforce

### Goal 4 — The changing healthcare environment
- 4.1 National health data resources and advanced analytics
- 4.2 Clinical cancer research as part of care
- 4.3 Personalised medicine
- 4.4 Multidisciplinary research
- 4.5 Consistent assessment and introduction of new cancer medicines and methods

## Relevance to Equal Care Navigator Across Finland

These are **project interpretations**, not source text:

- **Citizens:** plain-language prevention, screening and care navigation can address documented accessibility and health-literacy challenges.
- **Decision-makers:** compare available regional indicators, identify pathway bottlenecks and propose evidence-based organisational improvements.
- **Data governance:** clearly separate public aggregate indicators from restricted health records and report missing data.
- **AI safety:** suggestions should be supported by cited evidence and remain subject to human review.
